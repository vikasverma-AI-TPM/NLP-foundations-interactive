Turning text into numbersthat actually mean something

Every NLP system starts with the same problem: a machine can't read words, only numbers. This guide walks through the four ways text becomes numbers — from the simplest (a word is either present or it isn't) to the kind that actually understands meaning.

1
Encoding & Embedding

Encoding & Embedding

The foundations everything else in this guide sits on top of.

Vectors, scalars, and matrices

A scalar is a single number — 5. It has no direction, just magnitude. The moment you have a set of numbers together, like [5, 10], you have a vector. Stack several vectors on top of each other and you get a matrix — literally a table of numbers, or "a collection of vectors."

ObjectExampleShape
Scalar5—
Vector[1, 2, 3]1 × 3
Matrix[[1,2],[3,4]]2 × 2

In NLP, a vector is a numerical representation of data in n-dimensional space. An embedding is just a vector that additionally captures meaning — direction and magnitude that reflect semantic relationships, not just position.

One rung further up this ladder: stack multiple matrices together and you get a tensor — the general term for "array of numbers with any number of dimensions." A scalar is a 0-D tensor, a vector is 1-D, a matrix is 2-D. Frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow use this term because deep learning routinely works with 3-D, 4-D, and higher arrays (a batch of images, for instance, is a 4-D tensor: batch × height × width × color channels).

What encoding and embedding actually mean

Encoding = converting raw data into numerical form (often sparse), without necessarily preserving semantic meaning. It's count-based or matrix-factorization based: One-Hot Encoding, Bag of Words, TF-IDF, N-grams.

Embedding = a numerical representation that's dense, high-dimensional (typically 100 to a few thousand dimensions: 384, 768, 1024, 1536, 3072...), and learned by a neural network or transformer rather than counted directly.

The cleaner way to say this: encoding is a sparse, explicit representation — you can point to exactly why each number is what it is. Embedding is a dense, learned representation — the numbers come out of training, not a counting rule. An embedding is still technically a kind of encoding; it's just one where the numbers are learned rather than assigned by a fixed formula.

Embeddings don't "contain" meaning the way a dictionary entry does — but semantic relationships tend to emerge geometrically from training on large amounts of text. A useful gut-check: embedding("king") vs. embedding("queen") → small angle, small distance (similar usage contexts). embedding("king") vs. embedding("banana") → large angle, large distance. Encoded vectors (OHE/BoW/TF-IDF) have no such property — two unrelated words can sit at the exact same distance as two synonyms.

Encoding vs. embedding, side by side

The table in the previous version of this guide said "semantic meaning: not preserved / preserved" and "context awareness: not preserved / preserved" — too absolute. TF-IDF does carry useful statistical signal (that's the whole point of it), and Word2Vec — a genuine embedding — is not context-aware in the way modern transformer models are. The more accurate framing is about what's usual, not a hard binary:

FeatureSparse / explicit representationLearned embedding
RepresentationUsually sparseUsually dense
How values ariseRule- or count-basedLearned from data
Semantic relationshipsLimited / indirectOften emerge from training
Context awarenessUsually limitedDepends on the specific model
ExampleOHE, BoW, TF-IDFWord2Vec, BERT, modern embedding models

Similarity, semantic search & where this is used

Once data is a vector, "how similar are these two things?" becomes a geometry question, answered with cosine similarity, dot product, or Euclidean distance. This is the entire basis of semantic search — matching by meaning instead of exact words.

Keyword searchSemantic search
Matches exact wordsMatches meaning via vector similarity
"reduce heart risk" ≠ "lower cardiovascular disease"Correctly treats them as near-identical
Weak on paraphrasingStrong on paraphrasing

Applications that all reduce to "find the nearest vector": Google-style search, image search (Pinterest, "a red car"), recommendations (Netflix, Amazon, Spotify), topic clustering, and RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) — retrieving relevant chunks before an LLM answers.

One-Hot Encoding

One-Hot Encoding

"Is the word present, or not?" — the simplest possible answer to "text → numbers."

The idea

Build a vocabulary — the set of unique words across all your documents. Every document then becomes a vector as long as the vocabulary, with a 1 where a word is present and 0 everywhere else. Values are strictly 0 or 1 — presence, not frequency.

A note on terminology: strict one-hot encoding means exactly one 1 in the whole vector — that's how you'd encode a single token, or a single categorical label. The document-level example below has multiple 1s per row (one per word present in that document), which makes it technically a multi-hot / binary presence vector, not strictly one-hot. In practice the two terms get used loosely and interchangeably — worth knowing the distinction, especially if it comes up in an interview.

Worked example (from notes)

Four documents:

D1people watch movie
D2people watch cricket
D3people like movie
D4people like cricket

Vocabulary (5 unique words): {people, watch, like, movie, cricket} — this also fixes the vector's dimension at 5.

peoplewatchlikemoviecricket
D111010
D211001
D310110
D410101

D1 → [1,1,0,1,0], D2 → [1,1,0,0,1], and so on. This is the document-level representation. A word-level (sequence) version also exists — each word in a sentence becomes its own one-hot row, producing a matrix like 3×5 for a 3-word sentence — used as the raw input format feeding into sequence models (RNN / LSTM / GRU / attention) before embeddings took over.

Pros & cons

Pros

  • Easy to implement, no training required
  • Simple binary representation — direct mapping

Cons

  • Sparse & memory-wasteful — a 10,000-word vocabulary means a vector like [1,1,0,1,0,0,...] with 9,997 zeros for a 3-word document
  • High dimensionality — vector size scales directly with vocabulary size
  • No semantic understanding — "like" and "love" are as unrelated as "like" and "airplane"
  • OOV (out-of-vocabulary) problem — D5 "people enjoy movie": enjoy was never in the vocabulary, so it simply can't be represented

Bag of Words

Bag of Words

"How many times does this word appear?" — frequency as a stand-in for importance.

The idea

Same vocabulary-based structure as OHE, except each dimension now holds a count instead of a 0/1 flag. The underlying assumption — "a word that repeats more is more important" — is at best a crude proxy for actual importance, not a reliable one. It ignores which word is repeating and why.

Worked example (from notes)

D1people watch movie and watch movie again
D2people watch cricket and watch cricket
D3people like movie and like movie a lot
D4people like cricket

Vocabulary (8 words): [again, and, cricket, like, lot, movie, people, watch]

againandcricketlikelotmoviepeoplewatch

Pros & cons

Pros

  • Easy to implement, no training required
  • Captures word frequency — useful for basic spam detection, text classification, sentiment (basic)

Cons

  • Ignores word order — "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce the identical vector
  • No semantic understanding — "like" and "love" share no relation in the vector
  • Sparse, high-dimensional — vocabulary of 50,000 words means a 50,000-length vector, mostly zeros
  • OOV problem — same as OHE
  • Overemphasizes frequent words — "movie movie movie" scores high importance despite carrying no real meaning

TF-IDF

TF-IDF

Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency — "how important is this word, really?"

The formula

TF(word, D)= (occurrences of word in D) / (total words in D)
IDF(word)= log( total number of documents / documents containing the word )
TF-IDF(word, D)= TF(word, D) × IDF(word)

TF tells you how important a word is within one document. IDF tells you how important (i.e. rare) that word is across the whole corpus — common words are penalized, rare words are boosted. Multiplying the two gives a score that balances how frequently a term appears in this document against how rare it is across the corpus.

Why the log in IDF?

Without a log, IDF = N/DF produces wildly extreme values. Assume 1,000 total documents:

Word appears inN/DFlog(N/DF)
1 document (rare)1000≈ 6.9
10 documents100≈ 4.6
500 documents (common)2≈ 0.69

Without the log, 1000 vs. 100 vs. 2 lets one rare word's score swamp every other word's by two to three orders of magnitude. The log compresses that range while preserving the same order — rare words still score higher than common ones, just not by an absurd, disproportionate margin.

Worked example (from notes)

D1people watch cricket
D2cricket watch cricket
D3people give comment
D4cricket give comment

Vocabulary: [comment, cricket, give, people, watch]. Final TF-IDF matrix (natural log, rounded to 3 decimals):

commentcricketgivepeoplewatch
D100.09600.2310.231
D200.191000.231
D30.23100.2310.2310
D40.2310.0960.23100

Notice "cricket" scores lower than "people," "give," or "watch" even though it appears more often overall — because it also appears in more documents, so IDF pulls its weight down. That's TF-IDF working as intended: frequent-everywhere ≠ important.

Where it's used

Search engines and information retrieval (an early ranking signal, later extended by algorithms like BM25, a ranking method built on top of TF-IDF ideas), document ranking, and as a lightweight feature set for classical ML text classifiers (Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression) before embeddings became standard.

Word2Vec

Word2Vec

Google, 2013. The first method in this guide that actually learns meaning, not just counts.

The idea

The throughline for this whole guide: sparse representation → dense representation → semantic relationships. OHE, BoW, and TF-IDF are all sparse and count-based — no meaning, just presence or frequency. Word2Vec is the first method here that's dense and learned — and semantic structure falls out of that learning process.

Every method so far treats words as isolated symbols — OHE, BoW, and TF-IDF all produce "like" ≠ "love". Word2Vec instead trains a small neural network to predict words from their surrounding context, and the by-product of that training — the learned weights — becomes each word's embedding. The result: "like" ≈ "love", "movie" ≈ "film".

The whole approach rests on one idea, the distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meaning. Word2Vec doesn't have any dictionary of definitions — it only ever sees which words show up near which other words, millions of times over. Everything else (synonyms clustering together, king−man+woman≈queen) is a consequence of that one assumption holding up well in practice.

Word2Vec comes in two architectures, both defined by how training pairs are built from raw text:

CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)Skip-gram
Input: surrounding context wordsInput: the target (center) word
Output: the target (center) wordOutput: the surrounding context words
Faster to train, better for frequent wordsSlower, better for rare words

Each individual context→target (or target→context) pair is one training example — but "one pair = one forward pass" is a simplification. In practice, examples are usually grouped into small mini-batches (say, 32 or 256 pairs at once) and processed together for efficiency, with weights updated once per batch rather than once per single pair. The underlying idea is identical either way — this is purely an implementation detail for speed.

Window size & context

The window size decides how many neighboring words count as "context" around a target word. For I love deep learning with window size 1, the target love has context {I, deep}. With window size 2, its context grows to {I, deep, learning}. Note the target at either edge of a sentence simply has a smaller context — I only ever has words to its right, since there's nothing to its left. Context windows don't cross sentence boundaries either: the last word of one sentence isn't treated as context for the first word of the next.

Window size is a hyperparameter — commonly tried at 5, 10, 15, 20. Push it too high and context keeps growing, but so does noise: distant, less-related words start diluting the genuinely close relationships you actually want captured.

CBOW vs. Skip-gram — compared

The concept section already named the two architectures. Here's where the difference actually bites: they aren't just "different data layout," they train differently, and that produces different quality trade-offs — especially for rare words.

PropertyCBOWSkip-gram
SpeedFastSlow
Typical qualityGood — solid on frequent wordsOften better on rare words
Rare wordsWeakStrong
Data requirementLessMore
PartCBOWSkip-gram
Input vectorsMultiple (all context words)Single (the center word)
Hidden layerAverage of the input vectorsDirect pass-through
OutputSingle predictionMultiple predictions

CBOW takes several context words in at once, averages them at the hidden layer, and predicts one target — one forward pass, one loss value, done. Skip-gram flips it: one center word goes in, and the network has to separately predict each context word — so for a window of 2, that's effectively multiple predictions and multiple losses computed per training example, not one. That's the direct reason Skip-gram is slower: it produces more training signal per sentence, at higher compute cost per sentence.

Why Skip-gram wins on rare words: take the sentence "The king drank wine", with king as the word of interest.

In CBOW, the training pair is (The, drank, wine) → king. king only ever appears as the output to be predicted — its own embedding gets updated only indirectly, through the error signal flowing back from one prediction.

In Skip-gram, king is the input, generating three separate training pairs: king → The, king → drank, king → wine. king's embedding gets directly, repeatedly updated — one update per context word, every time it appears anywhere in the corpus. For a word that doesn't show up often, that repeated direct exposure is what gives Skip-gram the stronger, more reliable embedding — CBOW simply doesn't get enough indirect signal to learn a rare word well.

Neural network basics

Word2Vec's "neural network" is less intimidating than it sounds: a small, fully-connected network with exactly one hidden layer. Understanding it here also explains what's happening — at a much larger scale — inside every transformer model down the line.

One thing worth holding onto before the layer-by-layer detail below: the word-prediction task is never the actual goal. It's just the exercise that forces the network to learn something useful. What the network actually learns, over the course of training, is a set of weights — and those learned weights are exactly what become each word's embedding. Everything that follows is really just explaining how that learning happens.

The three layers

LayerWhat it holds
Input layerOne neuron per vocabulary word, fed as a one-hot vector — exactly one neuron is "1," the rest are "0"
Hidden layerIts size (e.g. 300 neurons) is a hyperparameter you choose. Once training finishes, this layer's weight matrix — one row per word — is the embedding table
Output layerSame size as the vocabulary again — a probability score for every word, i.e. the network's guess for what belongs in that slot

Forward propagation (the forward pass)

Every connection between two neurons carries a weight. At each neuron: multiply every incoming value by its weight, sum them, add a bias, then pass the result through an activation function — in short, Activation(W·x + b). Do this layer by layer, left to right, until you reach the output layer. That entire left-to-right pass is one forward pass.

Loss

Compare the output layer's prediction against the actual target word. The gap between them is the loss — a single number that says how wrong the guess was. High loss = the network's current weights are far from correct for this example. For a prediction task like this (predicting one word out of a vocabulary), the specific loss function is almost always cross-entropy — also called negative log-likelihood — which penalizes the network more heavily the more confidently wrong it was.

Backward propagation & epoch

The backward pass (backpropagation) takes that loss and works right-to-left back through the network, calculating how much each individual weight contributed to the error. An optimizer (commonly gradient descent) then nudges every weight slightly in the direction that reduces the loss.

Epoch, precisely: one epoch = one complete pass through the entire training dataset — not a single forward+backward pass. Within that one epoch, many individual forward/backward passes happen — one per training example, or one per mini-batch if examples are processed in small groups — each one nudging the weights a little. Weights start random → train for many epochs → loss keeps dropping → you converge on the best possible set of weights. For Word2Vec specifically, that final hidden-layer weight matrix is the embedding table used everywhere else in this guide.

Negative sampling

There's a practical problem with training the way described above: the output layer is the size of the entire vocabulary. For every single training pair, computing a full probability distribution over 50,000+ words (softmax) and backpropagating through all of them is extremely expensive — and most of those words had nothing to do with the current example anyway.

Negative sampling is the fix Word2Vec actually uses in practice. Instead of updating weights for the entire vocabulary on every step:

  • Keep the one real positive pair (the actual context/target word) — the network should learn to score this high.
  • Randomly sample a handful (typically 5–20) of negative pairs — words that were not actually nearby — and train the network to score these low instead.
  • Update weights only for those few words, not all 50,000+.

This turns an expensive "which one of 50,000 words is correct?" problem into a cheap "is this specific word right or wrong?" problem, repeated a handful of times — orders of magnitude faster, with barely any loss in embedding quality. A related, less commonly used alternative is hierarchical softmax, which organizes the vocabulary into a binary tree so a prediction only needs to walk down one path of that tree (roughly log(V) steps) instead of scoring every word — both approaches solve the same scalability problem, just differently.

How this applies to Word2Vec specifically

Each training pair (context → target for CBOW, or target → context for Skip-gram) is one forward-pass example. The network predicts a word, compares it to the actual target, and backpropagates the error — exactly as described above. The only Word2Vec-specific choice is the hidden layer size: pick 300 neurons, and you get 300-dimensional embeddings (Google's pretrained vectors use exactly this). The prediction task itself is never used again after training — it was only ever the mechanism for forcing the hidden layer to learn useful weights.

Why "hidden layer = embedding" is actually true — the matrix view

This is the part that's easy to state but not obvious why it's true. Let the weight matrix between the input and hidden layer be W, with shape V × D (V = vocabulary size, D = embedding dimension). A one-hot input vector x has a single 1 at the position of one word and zeros everywhere else.

x · W = hW ∈ ℝ^(V×D), x is one-hot (1×V), h is the word's embedding (1×D)

With actual numbers: a 10,000-word vocabulary and a 300-dimensional embedding gives x shape 1 × 10,000, W shape 10,000 × 300, and the product xW = h shape 1 × 300 — a 300-dimensional vector. That's the word's embedding.

Multiplying a one-hot vector by W zeroes out every row of W except the one matching that word's position — the matrix multiplication reduces to picking out a single row of W. That's the whole trick: the "hidden layer computation" for a one-hot input is mathematically identical to an embedding lookup. This is exactly why production systems don't bother doing the matrix multiplication at all — they just index directly into the embedding table.

The pipeline this sets up, which carries all the way into modern LLMs: text → tokens → token IDs → embedding lookup → vectors. Word2Vec's one-hot-times-matrix is the simple version of this. Modern transformer models (BERT, GPT) use the exact same lookup step, then add something Word2Vec doesn't have — context-dependent vectors, where the embedding for a word shifts based on the sentence around it. That's the next layer of this story, beyond what these four classes covered.

Vector arithmetic — a demonstration, not a proof

The clearest illustration that Word2Vec embeddings encode relational structure, not just similarity, is that they support arithmetic. The textbook case is King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen — worth treating as a famous, genuinely useful demonstration of how relationships (like "royalty" or "gender") sit along consistent directions in the embedding space, rather than as formal proof that embeddings "understand" meaning. Your notes illustrate this with a simplified, manually-assigned 5-feature table (Gender, Wealth, Power, Weight, Speak) — not real trained embeddings, but useful for building intuition:

GenderWealthPowerWeightSpeak
King110.990.71
Man10.50.40.71
Woman10.40.30.81
Queen (actual)10.80.70.81
worked out King − Man + Woman = [1−1+1, 1−0.5+0.4, 0.99−0.4+0.3, 0.7−0.7+0.8, 1−1+1] = [1, 0.9, 0.89, 0.8, 1] — versus Queen's actual [1, 0.8, 0.7, 0.8, 1]. Close, but not exact. That gap is expected: this table's numbers were assigned by hand as a teaching aid, not learned from data. Real Word2Vec embeddings (300 dimensions, learned from millions of sentences) reproduce this king–man+woman≈queen pattern far more precisely — the manual version just makes the underlying logic visible.

Training your own Word2Vec model

Everything above explains Google's pretrained vectors. In practice you'll often need a model trained on your own text — internal documents, a specific domain vocabulary, a language Google didn't cover well — using a library like gensim. The theory is identical to what's already been covered; this is that same process laid out as a concrete workflow.

1. Prepare the corpus

Raw text first needs to become a list of tokenized sentences — Word2Vec never sees paragraphs or documents, only sequences of words. This means: split text into sentences (e.g. sent_tokenize), then split each sentence into a clean list of lowercase word tokens (e.g. simple_preprocess, which also strips punctuation and very short tokens). The end result is the exact shape Word2Vec expects: a list of sentences, each sentence a list of words — e.g. [['ai', 'is', 'the', 'future'], ['farmers', 'grow', 'crops'], ...].

2. Set the hyperparameters

ParameterWhat it controls
vector_sizeThe embedding dimension — how many numbers represent each word (e.g. 150). Same idea as the hidden-layer-size discussed above.
windowContext window size — same concept covered earlier in this page.
min_countDiscard any word that appears fewer than this many times in the whole corpus. This isn't because such a word is inherently unable to have meaning — it's that too few occurrences means too few training pairs, so the gradient updates for that word stay too sparse and noisy to converge on anything useful (the same underlying signal shortage as the CBOW-vs-Skip-gram rare-word issue above, just handled here by exclusion rather than architecture choice).
sgArchitecture switch — sg=0 trains CBOW (the default), sg=1 trains Skip-gram.

3. Build the vocabulary, then train

Building the vocabulary is a separate step from training: the model first scans the entire corpus once to work out its full vocabulary (every unique word passing the min_count threshold) and initializes a random embedding for each. Only after that does actual training run — multiple epochs over the corpus, each one performing the forward/backward passes described earlier for every context→target (or target→context) pair, gradually adjusting those random starting vectors toward useful ones.

Nothing here is a new concept — it's the same input layer → hidden layer → output layer → loss → backprop → epoch loop from the "Neural network basics" section above, just running against your own text instead of Google's. What's different is only the choice of corpus and hyperparameters.

4. Use the trained model

Once training finishes, the model's hidden-layer weight matrix — the embedding table — can be queried directly:

  • Look up a word's raw vector — a list of vector_size numbers
  • Find the words closest to a given word by cosine similarity (most similar)
  • Score how similar two specific words are to each other (similarity)
  • Pick the odd one out of a list of words (doesn't match) — the word whose vector sits furthest from the others

All four of these are just vector-space arithmetic on the embedding table — nearest-neighbor lookups and cosine-distance comparisons — nothing the model does "specially" at query time. The intelligence is entirely baked into the vectors themselves by the time training is done.

practical note A model trained on a small custom corpus (a handful of documents) will have noticeably rougher embeddings than Google's pretrained vectors, which were trained on billions of words. That's expected, not a bug — quality here scales directly with corpus size and epoch count. The usual next steps for improving a custom model: add more training data, increase epochs, and compare results at different vector_size settings.

Pros & cons

Pros

  • Efficient & fast — a shallow network, no deep layers
  • Semantic understanding — king ≈ queen, car ≈ vehicle
  • Vector arithmetic — king − man + woman ≈ queen
  • Pretrained embeddings available (e.g. Google News 300-dim) — reusable without retraining
  • General-purpose: usable in NLP pipelines, classical ML models, clustering, search

Cons

  • Context-independent — "bank" gets the same vector whether it means a riverbank or a finance bank
  • Static embeddings — fixed once trained, can't adapt to sentence context
  • OOV problem — a brand-new word has no vector at all
  • No subword understanding — "running," "runner," "runs" are treated as unrelated tokens (FastText improved on this by averaging subword vectors)
  • Superseded for context-sensitivity by transformer-based models (BERT, GPT), which generate a different vector for "bank" depending on the sentence
